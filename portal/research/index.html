Research

PharmD, PhD student in pharmacology. I work on model-informed precision dosing: mechanistic pharmacokinetic models fitted to an individual patient's measured concentrations, used to support a dosing decision. Version française.

The question

Two patients given the same dose of the same drug do not reach the same blood concentration. A mathematical model of what the body does to the drug can be fitted to the handful of concentrations measured in one patient, and then used to propose a dose for that patient. This is routine clinical practice in transplantation, intensive care and oncology.

What limits these models in practice is not computation. It is the human work — slow, and poorly reproducible — of building them, tuning them, and documenting the assumptions they rest on.

An equation that changes no prescription has produced nothing. My doctoral work asks one question: what can safely be handed to a language model inside that model-building loop, and where exactly does the line fall beyond which it must not go?

In one sentence

I am testing whether a large language model can help build the mathematical models used to choose a drug dose for a given patient, and how far it can be trusted: the language model is a layer of assembly, parameterisation and documentation — not a substitute for structure.

For a scientist from another field

Depending on the drug, exposure between two patients on the same dose can differ by a factor of two to a factor of ten. Pharmacometrics models that variability: a system of differential equations describes the fate of the drug in the body, a statistical model describes how its parameters vary between individuals, and the whole is fitted to the few concentrations measured in one patient in order to propose a dose.

The models work. What does not scale is how they are made: choosing the structure, stating the assumptions, screening covariates, writing the code, then documenting and justifying every decision. That work is textual, carried out by a small number of specialists, and rarely reproducible exactly. It is precisely the class of task large language models are good at — and also the class of task on which they fail in subtle, hard-to-detect ways. My work sits at that intersection: not replacing the mechanistic model, but establishing what a language model can do around it without degrading the reliability of the dosing decision.

The field's name for the object being built is a digital pharmacological twin. It is best defined negatively: it is not an avatar of the patient, and it is not a prediction extracted from an electronic health record. It is an individualised pharmacological model, updated by that patient's data, and capable of answering questions about a dose that has not yet been given.

For a peer

The base object remains a nonlinear mixed-effects model: an ODE system for the kinetics, an inter-individual variability model, a residual error model, and maximum a posteriori Bayesian estimation for individualisation. What limits scale is not the solver but the construction loop — prior elicitation from the literature, covariate hypothesis generation and testing, choice of residual error structure, control-stream authoring, and the written credibility argument: question of interest, context of use, risk level, declared domain of validity. That loop is textual, slow and weakly reproducible.

Hybrid modelling as currently documented in pharmacometrics acts on the vector field itself: neural ODEs when the whole field is learned, universal differential equations when known mechanistic terms are preserved and the network only carries the uncertain ones. My work addresses a different layer. The language model does not enter the vector field; it operates in the construction and documentation loop, where being textual is an asset rather than a defect.

Two difficulties shape the work. The evaluation endpoint. It cannot be "the model writes a correct control stream", which is a self-congratulatory metric. The endpoint is the decision: does the proposed dose change, is the uncertainty around it propagated correctly, does the argument remain auditable by a third party? The boundary. Structural and practical identifiability cannot be delegated; nothing in a language model guarantees it, and a language model is particularly good at producing a plausible artefact for a non-identifiable problem. My experimental entry point is the residual error model, which I have argued elsewhere is an underused lever in model-informed precision dosing. The reference vocabulary is that of model-informed drug development (MIDD) as fixed by the ICH M15 guideline: context of use, and risk-proportionate credibility assessment.

What this work is not

Four clarifications. They delimit the subject more efficiently than a definition would, and they restate the criticisms the field addresses to itself.

It is not a clinical decision tool.

Nothing described here is intended, as it stands, to produce a dose for a real patient. The work concerns how a model is built, tuned and documented — upstream of any clinical use, and under the supervision of a pharmacologist.

It is not a deployed system.

There is no software in service, no hospital installation, no user other than me. What exists is an evaluation protocol and methodological experiments. An entry will appear under "Work" once it has a public, verifiable trace — not before.

It is not automated prediction from patient records.

Some work predicts the trajectory of clinical variables directly from electronic health record data, with no mechanistic model. That is statistical forecasting: the dose–exposure–response relationship is absent, so the model cannot be queried about a dose that was never given. That absence is exactly what separates a digital pharmacological twin from those approaches.

It is not a paradigm shift.

The literature itself points out that most properties attributed to digital pharmacological twins — individual simulation, sequential updating, decision-oriented uncertainty quantification — belong to established pharmacometrics, and that identifiability keeps imposing hard limits. That criticism is sound. It sets the bar this work has to clear to be worth anything.

Where this work sits

I carry out this doctoral work at INSERM UMR 1248 "Pharmacology and Transplantation", a joint research unit of the University of Limoges, INSERM and Limoges University Hospital, in Limoges, France. My thesis is supervised by Prof. Jean-Baptiste Woillard, who is also deputy director of the unit; the unit itself is directed by Prof. Pierre Marquet. Its stated areas include pharmacokinetic modelling and personalised medicine, and AI-augmented pharmacology; it develops and maintains the expert system for Bayesian dose adaptation of immunosuppressants used by transplant centres.

I am a member of the DIGPHAT consortium on digital pharmacological twins, which brings together around ten French institutions across three use cases: transplantation, infectious diseases and oncology. The consortium's conceptual framework is set out in Woillard JB et al., Therapie, 2026 — doi.org/10.1016/j.therap.2025.09.006.

Open questions

What I do not know yet, and what the current work is organised around.

How do you evaluate an assistant that does not output a number?

Scoring the correctness of generated code is a self-congratulatory metric: easy to obtain, and silent about use. The relevant endpoint concerns the decision — does the proposed dose change, does build time change, can a third party reconstruct the argument? I have no satisfactory protocol for the third yet.

What must never be delegated?

Structural and practical identifiability is the strongest candidate: it is exactly where a language model produces a plausible, wrong artefact with no warning signal. Whether that boundary can be made operational — that is, checked automatically — or whether it remains irreducibly a matter of human expertise, is open.

Can a credibility argument stay auditable if part of the model was assembled by a machine?

ICH M15 requires the context of use, the domain of validity and the risk level to be documented. A partly automated pipeline has to produce that documentation, not bypass it. This may be the most solid contribution a language model can make, and it is the least spectacular.

What follows from pharmacometrics being thinly represented in training corpora?

General-purpose models have seen very little NONMEM control-stream code, few modelling reports and little of the domain literature. That is a structural constraint rather than a passing defect, and it bounds what can be expected from retrieval grounding or fine-tuning.

Is the residual error model a generalisable entry point?

It is my experimental entry point, because its effect on estimated exposure is measurable and because it is rarely discussed. Whether what holds for residual error also holds for other construction decisions, or whether it is a convenient special case, is still open.
